### Primary theorem and exact substitution

Massey, *Shift-Register Synthesis and BCH Decoding* (1969), §IV, Theorem 4 and corollary, printed pp.125–126 (PDF pages 4–5), identifies the sequences generated by a connection polynomial C(D) with rational series P(D)/C(D), deg P < length. Its §V equations (26)–(27) and Theorem 5 give the modal partial-fraction connection for nonzero BCH error magnitudes. The normalized reduced denominator governs minimal register length, rather than the count of nonzero taps. Source: https://www.isiweb.ee.ethz.ch/archive/massey_pub/pdf/BI411.pdf

Here is the exact comparison, derived directly from the owner's definitions. Work over a field K with N>=1, k>=1, supplied distinct nonzero z_s and arbitrary amplitudes a_s. Define

S(t)=sum_(m>=0) f_m t^m=sum_s a_s/(1-z_s t),
f_m=sum_s a_s z_s^m,
Q(t)=product_s(1-z_s t).

For a positive stride b, let Lambda_b be the distinct z_s^b, let d_b=|Lambda_b|, R_b(u)=product_(lambda in Lambda_b)(1-lambda u), and C_b(t)=R_b(t^b). Since C_b(z_s^-1)=0 for every s and the original roots are distinct, Q divides C_b. Thus C_b S is a polynomial of degree < b d_b. Massey's C is exactly C_b and its register length is b d_b. Taking coefficients gives the owner's recurrence

f_m=-sum_(i:r_i!=0) r_i f_(m-bi), for m>=b d_b.

This argument requires no separability of t^b and no characteristic restriction on b. Powered nodes may merge and are deduplicated. Nonzero nodes imply r_(d_b)!=0, so at least one tap remains. This is an all-amplitude identity, not a finite-prefix membership guess.

Important qualification: Q is the common annihilator for the supplied support. It is the reduced denominator of an individual input only after inactive zero-amplitude modes are removed. An all-zero input has reduced denominator 1. Claims about the shortest recurrence for each particular input cannot silently assume all supplied amplitudes are nonzero. The sparse-multiple evaluator is allowed to use a larger recurrence order to reduce its tap count; shortest order and cheapest full evaluator are different objectives.

### Sparse interpolation is an opposite input/output interface

Van der Hoeven–Lecerf, *Sparse polynomial interpolation: Faster strategies over finite fields*, final 2 May 2024, §5 Algorithm 4 (printed pp.13–14), starts from black-box polynomial evaluations and degree/term bounds. It obtains a rational denominator, finds roots, recovers exponents by discrete logarithms, and then coefficients. For g(x)=sum_s a_s x^(e_s), the exact bridge is z_s=omega^(e_s) and g(omega^m)=f_m, with distinct residues and suitable-order guards. Algorithm 4 uses a monic denominator Lambda; the reduced active-mode denominator product_(a_s!=0)(1-z_s t) is proportional to Lambda, with the numerator scaled by the same nonzero factor. Its roots are reciprocal modal nodes. The owner instead uses a constant-one common support denominator and may choose a nonminimal multiple. ALG-014 starts after the modes and amplitudes are already supplied and outputs all N values. The recovery/preparation steps cannot be granted for free. The paper also separates field and bit costs and states heuristic assumptions for several faster strategies; none are imported as unconditional guarantees. ### Complex matrix-function correspondence

Higham–Lin, *Matrix Functions: A Short Course* (2013), Definition 3.2 (printed p.5), identifies f(T) with its Hermite interpolating polynomial modulo the minimal polynomial; Theorem 3.4 and §3.2 (pp.7–8) give the similarity, spectral and functional relations. Those statements are over C. Map their matrix to M and f(z)=1/(1+z), or to K and f(z)=1/(1-z), away from the relevant pole. The accepted cubic makes the inverse a polynomial of degree at most two. This identifies the classical matrix-function mechanism; it does not print or establish historical priority for the complete chirp family. Source: https://eprints.maths.manchester.ac.uk/2067/1/covered/MIMS_ep2013_73.pdf

The following algebra is a direct check over the owner's field, not a transfer of complex spectral theory. Since

(1+x)(1-x+x^2)=1+x^3,

M^3=cI and 1+c!=0 give (I+M)^-1=(I-M+M^2)/(1+c). Set

B=(I+M)^-1 F,
K=BA=M(I+M)^-1.

Then I-K=(I+M)^-1 and (I-K)^-1 B=F. Clearing the Möbius relation yields

mu(z)=z^3-c(1-z)^3,
mu(K)=0,
mu(1)=1.

For q(z)=(1+c)+(1-2c)z+(1+c)z^2, direct multiplication gives

(1-z)q(z)=1-mu(z),

hence (I-K)^-1=q(K). This proof does not divide by 3, require cube roots, diagonalizability or distinct eigenvalues. Characteristic 3 is allowed when the original 2N guard allows it; the cubic is then inseparable. Spectral-projector formulas using distinct cubic roots require the extra splitting and characteristic-not-3 assumptions. Over a splitting field with three distinct roots lambda_j of x^3-c, the usual products E_j=product_(l!=j)(M-lambda_l I)/(lambda_j-lambda_l) give the alternative inverse sum E_j/(1+lambda_j). These projectors contain M and M^2 and supply no cheap application by themselves.

Davies–Higham, *A Schur–Parlett Algorithm for Computing Matrix Functions* (2003), original author-deposited paper, §1 and Algorithm 5.1 (manuscript p.13), starts with a complex Schur decomposition and performs reordered/block triangular evaluations and Sylvester solves. It charges dense preparation, with generic cubic or worse matrix work. Its analytic-domain and derivative/Taylor requirements must be checked; a reciprocal function is not entire, and a nonsingular resolvent alone does not verify the algorithm's closed-convex-domain hypothesis. This is a qualified computational nonmatch to an exact supplied-root O(N) Green constructor, not a lower bound on every possible specialized algorithm. Source: https://eprints.maths.manchester.ac.uk/156/1/paper.pdf

### Fourier dependency and remaining gate

The source formula is

B=[F-FAF+c A^-1]/(1+c).

Thus its displayed implementation contains F, including an application needed before a later F inside FAF. The alternative Fbar formula still contains Fourier operators. Writing this inverse algebraically, diagonalizing M, or forming spectral projectors does not discharge their preparation/application costs. The conditional Horner realization uses three B calls, two diagonal A calls and vector work; it is fast only after an independent uniformly cheap B has been supplied. Full rank of B-A^-1 is a statement about that particular correction and is not an arithmetic lower bound against all algorithms.
