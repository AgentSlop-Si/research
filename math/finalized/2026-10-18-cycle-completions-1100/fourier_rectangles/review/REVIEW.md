## 1. Stable rank and the balanced-divisor proof

For the unnormalized Fourier matrix, `F*F = N I`. Row and column restrictions are contractions, so every singular value of an a-by-b rectangle is at most sqrt(N). Its squared Frobenius norm is ab, giving `ab <= Nr`. There is no missing normalization factor. Nonempty Fourier submatrices have nonzero entries, hence positive integer rank.

For rank at least two, AM-GM and stable rank give

`r(a+b)/(ab) >= 2r/sqrt(ab) >= 2sqrt(r/N) >= 2sqrt(2/N)`.

All inequality directions are correct. Separating rank one from integer ranks at least two is essential. For rank one, taking H generated by row differences gives the envelope `R subset k0+H`, `C subset j0+H-perp`. With `d=|H|`, the monotonic inequality

`1/a + 1/b >= 1/d + d/N >= D_N/N`

is valid. Equality requires both side sets to fill their cosets and d to minimize `d+N/d`. No equality cancellation is possible between tiles because every tile's local slack is nonnegative.

When `D_N^2 < 8N`, the density for rank at least two is strictly greater than `D_N/N`. Summing against tile areas, whose total is exactly `N^2`, proves both the lower bound and the exclusion of every higher-rank tile from an optimizer. Equality within rank one supplies the complete stated tilewise structure. This structure does not assert that every optimizer uses one globally fixed subgroup orientation, and the report correctly makes no such assertion.

The displayed coset partition attains the bound: the row classes partition Z_N, the annihilator column classes partition Z_N, their products partition all matrix entries, and their difference products are divisible by N. Each tile has area N and cost d+N/d.

The same summation directly gives formula (5), hence the near-optimality area estimate (6). Taking the smaller local density constant gives the universal sandwich. The divisor-ratio criterion is algebraically equivalent to `D_N^2 < 8N`; its equality boundary cannot occur at an integer divisor pair because it would require an irrational ratio. The prime-power formula and listed objective examples are correct.

## 2. Twice-prime proof and strict equality

Let N=2p, p odd prime, and transpose a rectangle if needed so that a<=b. The larger row parity class contains at least ceil(a/2) indices, and the larger column parity class at least ceil(b/2). Each class has at most p indices. In either parity, writing indices as `k0+2u` and `j0+2v` gives distinct u and v modulo p.

Expanding the product of indices shows that invertible row and column diagonal factors reduce the submatrix to entries `omega^(-4uv)`. The order of omega^4 is `(2p)/gcd(2p,4)=p`, using precisely that p is odd. Thus this is a submatrix of a prime-order complex Fourier matrix. Chebotarev's nonvanishing-minors theorem makes its rank its smaller side length. The whole rectangle has rank at least that, proving `r >= ceil(a/2)`.

The dependency is correctly stated. Theorem 1.1 of Garcia, Karaali and Katz, *An improved uncertainty principle for functions with symmetry*, explicitly supplies the nonvanishing-minors theorem for the complex Fourier matrix: [primary exposition, Section 1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1807.07648v4). I freshly read the theorem and the matrix definition. Unit normalization does not affect ranks, and changing a primitive root permutes rows. This review invokes the established theorem; it does not claim a new proof of Chebotarev.

The density cases are exhaustive:

- a=1 cannot have rank at least two.
- a=2 gives density at least `1+2/b >= 1+1/p`, strictly above `1/2+1/p`.
- a=3 gives at least `2/3+2/b >= 2/3+1/p`, again strictly above the target.
- a>=4 gives at least `1/2+a/(2b) >= 1/2+a/(4p) >= 1/2+1/p`.

Equality in the last chain requires simultaneously `r=a/2`, `b=2p`, and `a=4`. Its sole scalar candidate is `(a,b,r)=(4,2p,2)`. A submatrix containing all columns has independent rows because the Fourier matrix is invertible, so its actual rank is 4. This excludes that candidate for every odd prime, including p=3. Therefore every rank-at-least-two rectangle has strictly greater density, proving both the optimum and the same equality structure as in the balanced case.

This all-primes argument does not extrapolate from the bounded checks. It also does not need a finite-field analogue of Chebotarev or a finite-field operator norm.
