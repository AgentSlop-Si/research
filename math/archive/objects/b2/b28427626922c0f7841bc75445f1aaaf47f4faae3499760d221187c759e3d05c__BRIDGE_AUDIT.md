# Two additive Green sums bridge audit

## Conclusion

**Accept the bridge identities with their explicit invertibility assumptions.** They give an exact, denominator-cleared synthesis and verification obligation for a product of two additive Green sums. They do not produce Fourier parameters, prove that such parameters exist at any new size, improve FFT complexity, or exclude the architecture.

Two clarifications matter when integrating this section with the broader project:

1. The stated three-nonzero row/column bound for each sum applies to the literal denominators I minus weighted cyclic shifts, which share the identity support. Independent left/right monomial gauges preserve the general bridge identity but can increase each sum's bound to four; the simple determinant and adjugate expressions also need the gauge factors.
2. For the particular K=CB with its cyclic factors supplied, applying K inverse is two known cyclic solves and takes O(N) field operations. The unresolved task is synthesizing Fourier-producing factors and charging that construction. A warning about arbitrary sparse linear systems does not make this explicitly factored system hard to apply.

All independently authored exact controls passed, including order mutations, singular-denominator false acceptance, a determinant-only false acceptance and a gauged four-support example. Uploaded source code was neither run nor imported.

## Algebraic verification

Let A,B,C,D be invertible N-by-N matrices over a field. Define

    M = (A^(-1)+B^(-1))(C^(-1)+D^(-1)),
    X = A+B,  Y = C+D,  K = CB.

No commutativity is assumed. Direct multiplication gives

    A^(-1)+B^(-1) = A^(-1)(A+B)B^(-1),
    C^(-1)+D^(-1) = C^(-1)(C+D)D^(-1).

Thus

    A M D = X B^(-1) C^(-1) Y = X (CB)^(-1) Y.       (1)

The order CB is required because inversion reverses order. Substitution of BC is generally false; it happens to agree in special commuting or cancellation cases, so mutation rejection should not be asserted universally for every imaginable example.

Using adj(K)=det(K)K^(-1), the condition M=F_N is equivalent to

    det(CB) A F_N D = (A+B) adj(CB) (C+D),             (2)

**provided A,B,C,D are all invertible**. Equation (2) is necessary and sufficient in that domain: divide by det(CB), then multiply by A inverse and D inverse. Without the determinant guards it is not equivalent, since multiplying rational equations by a zero denominator can add spurious solutions.

The adjugate reverses product order too:

    adj(CB) = adj(B) adj(C).

Taking determinants of the original M gives the necessary scalar condition

    det(F_N) det(A) det(B) det(C) det(D)
        = det(A+B) det(C+D).                         (3)

It is far weaker than equation (2). If M=F_N and the four factors are invertible, both A+B and C+D must also be invertible. The bridge identity itself does not require the sums to be invertible; singular sums simply cannot produce an invertible Fourier matrix.

## Cyclic specialization and sparse representation

For a weighted Hamiltonian cyclic shift T, write A=I-T and lambda as the product of its N edge weights. Then

    T^N = lambda I,
    det(I-T) = 1-lambda,
    adj(I-T) = I+T+...+T^(N-1)

in the nonsingular domain. The last equality follows by multiplying the geometric sum by I-T and comparing with det(A)A inverse. Zero individual weights are allowed; lambda=1 is the prohibited case. Our controls include zero edges and independently verify the determinant, power and adjugate formulas.

For the literal denominators,

    A+B = 2I-T_A-T_B,
    C+D = 2I-T_C-T_D,
    CB = I-T_B-T_C+T_C T_B.

Each weighted permutation has at most one nonzero per row and column, so the first two have at most three and the last at most four. Cancellations may reduce these counts. These bounds hold in positive characteristic too, although characteristic two can cancel the diagonal term. They are support bounds, not a guarantee about the rank or exact Fourier phases.

With K invertible, the block matrix [[K,Y],[-X,0]] has Schur complement X K inverse Y. This is an O(N)-nonzero block representation when the indicated factors are supplied. Schur-complement terminology does not assert that arbitrary sparse systems can be solved rapidly or that the desired Fourier parameters can be constructed.

### Scope after independent gauges

A branch L G R, for invertible monomial L,R and G=(I-T) inverse, can still be written as an inverse denominator:

    (L G R)^(-1) = R^(-1)(I-T)L^(-1).

This denominator is at most two-sparse in rows and columns, but its first monomial need not be I or even supported on the main diagonal. Therefore the sum of two independently gauged denominators can have four nonzeros per row and column. K=CB remains at most four-sparse. The general identities (1)-(3) continue to hold, while det(A) includes det(R inverse)det(L inverse), and its adjugate must be transformed accordingly.

A concrete witness over GF(101), N=5, uses the standard shift S:

    A=I-2S,  B=(I-3S)S^2.

Both denominators are invertible. Their sum has the four distinct supports I,S,S^2,S^3 in every row and column. The independent checker verifies both that support count and the general bridge identity. This does not contradict the narrower three-support claim; it prevents silently transferring that count to the more general independently gauged architecture.

An explicit intermediate monomial routing can be absorbed into adjacent denominators, preserving the general two-sparse denominator class but not necessarily the literal I-minus-shift normalization. The source bridge alone therefore does not establish every detailed support/determinant claim for the earlier, broader model.

## Fourier identity and uniform construction

For a primitive Nth root omega, define the unnormalized Fourier matrix F[k,j]=omega^(kj), the shift S e_j=e_(j+1 mod N), and D_omega=diag(omega^k). The conditions

    M S = D_omega M,  M e_0 = ones

are necessary and sufficient for M=F: the jth column must be D_omega^j times the first. A unitary convention requires the first column ones/sqrt(N), and should not be interchanged with the unnormalized condition. Covariance alone permits a diagonal left scaling of Fourier; first-column agreement alone imposes no conditions on the remaining columns.

The bridge is thus a useful exact acceptance or elimination test for a proposed set of parameters. It does not supply those parameters. A uniform all-length construction would still need to specify the cycle permutations, all edge weights and gauges, root assumptions, a construction procedure and its full costs. Merely positing an O(N)-parameter formula or an O(N)-sparse block matrix gives none of those guarantees.

Equation (2) comprises N squared entry conditions if checked as a dense matrix identity. After substituting the cyclic adjugate formulas it is a polynomial system whose degrees grow with N. No bounded-degree, bounded-cost parameter solver or uniform family is furnished here. Numeric fitting, graph capacity and sampled entries cannot substitute for exact identities.

A nonzero exact-field specialization can refute a proposed universal algebraic identity when reduction respects the coefficient relations and denominators. It does not exclude an entire parameter chart merely because one point in that chart fails. A zero modular residue also cannot by itself certify a characteristic-zero identity. The source's random bridge checks neither construct Fourier nor eliminate every possible parameter choice.

## Costs and precision

Given one Hamiltonian cycle order, edge weights and right-hand side b, solving x_i-w_i x_succ(i)=b_i can be done in linear work without dividing by any individual weight. Following the cycle once computes a weighted sum beta and lambda, then x at one base point is beta/(1-lambda); a reverse traversal recovers the other coordinates. This works when some weights are zero.

The independent checker implements a deliberately simple version. Under unit-cost field arithmetic, including recomputation of lambda and its denominator each time, its counted operations are 3N multiplications, 2N additions/subtractions and one inversion per solve. Counts exclude modular representation costs, bit complexity and memory traffic. If the denominator inverse is precomputed, it must be charged to setup rather than treated as free.

Since (CB) inverse equals B inverse C inverse, applying the factored K inverse is two such solves. The original product of two sums uses four Green solves and two vector additions. The bridge reformulation also retains four outer/inner inverse factors and introduces sparse X,Y applications; it is not a demonstrated arithmetic saving. Unknown Fourier-producing coefficients, phase/root planning, permutations and data movement remain additional obligations.

Exact field identities say nothing by themselves about numerical conditioning. Small 1-lambda denominators and cancellation in sums can require high precision. Arbitrarily large exact coefficients and bit costs cannot be silently identified with stable bounded-precision O(N) work.

## Independent controls and false acceptance

`independent_checks.py` contains only reviewer-authored standard-library code. Its basic matrix arithmetic is reused from the independently authored sparse-prefix checker, not from the upload. It uses ordinary Gaussian elimination and, for small sizes, independent cofactor adjugates.

Results:

- 28 exact trials at N=2,3,5,7,8,11,13 over prime fields supporting primitive Nth roots.
- All 28 verify equation (1), recovery of M, denominator-cleared equation (2) with target M, determinant equation (3) with target M, the adjugate product order, and the claimed literal row/column support bounds.
- All 28 reject the reversed BC bridge in these selected noncommuting examples. All 28 reject Fourier equality despite satisfying the bridge identity, directly illustrating that the bridge alone does not synthesize Fourier.
- 12 small cofactor adjugate checks independently validate the determinant-times-inverse adjugates.
- 112 direct cyclic-solve controls include zero edges, plus 28 factored K solves, all checked against the full dense matrix equations.
- Covariance and first-column controls show that both Fourier conditions are needed.
- A monomial-gauged example attains four nonzeros per row and column in A+B while retaining the general bridge identity.

Two explicit false-accept controls are particularly important:

**Missing denominator guards.** At N=3 over GF(13), take A=D=I, B=I-S and C=I-3S. A,D are invertible, but B,C are singular and K has rank one. Its adjugate and determinant are both zero. Equation (2) becomes zero=zero for every target matrix, including Fourier, while M is undefined. Thus det(B)det(C) nonzero cannot be omitted from any polynomial feasibility test.

**Only the determinant condition.** At N=3 over GF(13), use constant cycle weights (0,0,2,5) for A,B,C,D and the primitive third root 3. All four factors are invertible. Their product of sums is

    M = [[5,11,1],[1,5,11],[11,1,5]],
    F = [[1,1,1],[1,3,9],[1,9,3]].

Both have determinant 5, so equation (3) passes, but M is not F. The full matrix equation rejects it.

The source's saved 20 bridge trials were inspected but were not rerun. The checks above are independently generated tests, not execution certification of `bridge.py`. No finite tests alone replace the general algebraic derivation.

## Acceptance and replay

Accepted: equations (1)-(3) in their stated domains, the cyclic specialization, the literal sparse-support bounds, and the Fourier covariance plus first-column characterization. Accepted as useful project bookkeeping: an exact synthesis obligation with noncommutative order and denominator guards exposed.

Not established: Fourier parameters, an exact faster FFT, an architecture impossibility theorem, a complete parameter-chart elimination or historical priority. The general gauged scope requires the support and gauge-factor qualifications above.
