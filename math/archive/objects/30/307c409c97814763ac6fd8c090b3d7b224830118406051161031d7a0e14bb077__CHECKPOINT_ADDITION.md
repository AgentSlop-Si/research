# Additive checkpoint: exact bounded-common-chirp recognition

**Date:** 9 October 2026. **Status:** original scoped theorem independently audited; polynomial-cost eliminant extension proved and tested. The original source note and original audit are preserved unchanged in this supplement.

## Correct theorem qualification

Let r≥1, N≥3r, p prime with N dividing p−1, and let a certified primitive Nth root omega in F_p be supplied. The raw-input recognizer accepts exactly sequences

    x[j] = omega^(a j²) sum_(ell=1)^s c_ell omega^(b_ell j), 0≤s≤r,

with distinct frequencies and nonzero active coefficients. The all-zero vector is the empty representation s=0 and is handled before searching for a nonzero interior witness. Rejection means only nonmembership in this family. For fixed r the recognition and accepted full-output transform take O_r(N) exact field arithmetic, including setup and output writes.

For a witness c=s_window+r with x[c]≠0, the phase eliminant P has degree **at most** D=binom(r+2,3), not necessarily exactly D, and constant term (−1)^(r(r+1)/2)x[c]^(r+1). The canonical phase count is L=N/gcd(2,N); at most C=min(D,L) phases survive. Different canonical phases may still represent the same signal, so uniqueness is not claimed.

## Constructive improvement

Replace factorial permutation expansion using

    A[i,j] = x[c+i−j] t^((i−j)(i−j−1)/2), 0≤i,j≤r.

Then P=(−1)^(r(r+1)/2)det A. A is polynomial Toeplitz, A(0) is lower bidiagonal with diagonal x[c], and every leading principal minor has nonzero constant term x[c]^k.

Two independently implemented routes follow:

- Exact polynomial Bareiss uses O(r³(D+1)²)=O(r⁹) field operations, at most r−1 scalar inversions, and O(r²(D+1)) workspace. Stored minors have degree at most D; multiplication results and division numerators have degree at most 2D.
- Nonsymmetric Levinson recursion in F_p[t]/(t^(D+1)), with Karatsuba multiplication and Newton series inversion, uses O(r²(D+1)^log₂3)=O(r^6.754888…) field operations, one scalar inversion, and O(r(D+1)) workspace. There is no division by an integer index and no requirement p>D.

Both return the same exact eliminant. The proof and code are in `eliminant_extension/`. These are applications of classical determinant, Toeplitz, and polynomial-arithmetic methods after the displayed normalization; no priority claim is made.

An optional ordinary-convolution scan evaluates P at the canonical geometric progression in

    S(N,r)=O(min{L(D+1), D+L^log₂3}).

Its coefficient folding, power table, and convolution are charged. With T(r) either construction bound, the unchanged candidate-recognition and verification steps give

    O(T(r)+S(N,r)+C(Nr+r³)+N).

The accepted full-output Gauss evaluation is covered by this upper bound. Memory becomes O(N+r(D+1)) for the Toeplitz route, or O(N+r²(D+1)) for Bareiss. These are linear in N only with r fixed. Candidate verification and growing-r costs remain; this is not a general O(N) Fourier algorithm.

## Independent evidence, kept separate

Original audit (`chirp_audit/`): 118,617 exhaustively classified finite-field vectors; 1,601 accepted; no false positive or false negative in those spaces. Further tests include 173,880 exact Gauss-sum entries, 252 structured recognition runs, 10,810 direct-DFT bins, 72 determinant polynomials, 696 scalar determinant evaluations, 810 dense rejections, and boundary/cancellation checks. These are new independent results, not a reproduction of unavailable historical source programs.

Extension (`eliminant_extension/`): eight passing test groups, with 210 direct-permutation coefficient comparisons, 388 scalar determinant comparisons, 108 structured true-phase checks, 3,311 geometric evaluation comparisons, 72 multiplication cross-checks, and 123 Toeplitz/Bareiss comparisons through rank 16. Valid p<D examples include N=12,p=13,r=4,D=20 and N=30,p=31,r=10,D=220. Characteristics 2 and 3 are extra determinant/arithmetic tests, not new admissible instances of the original N≥3r field theorem.

Both suites use independently written standard-library Python. Finite tests support implementation correctness; the general claims rest on the included proofs. Python timing is not an optimized FFT/NTT benchmark, and no floating-point stability or bit-complexity theorem is asserted. Prior-art review is focused and incomplete; common-rate chirp models and the classical ingredients are already established.
