# The user's proposal: make multiplication easy by replacing A with A-k

Date: 9 October 2026. **Verdict: a valid and useful special-case reduction;
no general worst-case improvement is established by this identity alone.**
This proposal can succeed universally if a cheap covering reduction is found.
The conditions below make that research target precise rather than dismissing it.

## 1. Where it immediately works

The identity is exact for every integer k, including negative k:

`A B = (A-k) B + k B`.

For `A=2^m+1`, take k=1; the product is `(B << m)+B`. For `A=2^m-1`,
take k=-1; the product is `(B << m)-B`. More generally, for
`A=2^m+k` and fixed bounded k, the shift, small-integer multiplication,
and addition all cost O(n) bit work when the operands and output have O(n) bits.
Reading the representation and detecting this pattern can also be O(n).
No FFT is required for those cases.

We implemented `adaptive_multiply` with at most four probes: the adjacent powers
of two for each operand, accepting correction magnitude at most 255. It computes
the correction, materializes the whole integer result, handles signs, and falls
back to the original multiplication when no acceptable representation is found.
Each timed call includes its own detector. There is no cache of previous products.

In this Python environment, 65,536-bit all-ones A times an unrelated dense B took
about 978.6 microseconds using `A*B`, versus 21.6 microseconds using the dispatcher:
45.3x in this local measurement. For `A=2^65536+13`, the ratio was 9.5x.
Alternating-bit and unrelated random cases used the fallback and were around
the original speed. At only 256 bits, dispatcher overhead made the prototype
roughly nine times slower. This is exploratory code, not a production threshold
policy. The baseline is Python integer multiplication, **not an FFT backend**;
these timings say nothing about an FFT exponent or performance against GMP.

## 2. The exact condition for upgrading fast cases to all cases

Fix an input-width bound n and the other operand B. Suppose:

1. Multiplication by A in a fast set `S_n` costs at most `T(n)` uniformly over B.
2. Every A in the n-bit input domain can be written `A=s+k`, with `s in S_n`
   and `k in K_n`.
3. We can find that decomposition in `R(n)` time.
4. Every permitted correction `kB` costs at most `Q(n)`.
5. The decomposed operands and output remain O(n) bits.

Then arbitrary multiplication costs

`T(n) + R(n) + Q(n) + O(n)`.

This is a real possible reduction. It beats `n log n` only if the *whole sum*
does, for every input. Finding k and calculating kB are part of the theorem.
Calling an arbitrary n-bit k "a constant" does not make its product cheap;
the bound on its representation must be uniform as n grows.

For example, if a genuinely faster all-size algorithm handled **every even A**,
then k in {0,1} would extend it to all A at linear correction cost. The usual
observation "even A can be shifted right by one" does not supply that algorithm:
it merely invokes multiplication on an operand one bit shorter. Repeating a
one-bit reduction with linear per-step work does not beat `n log n`.

This is the promising version of the proposal: find a large, efficiently
recognizable fast family with a cheap additive covering set. Sparse examples
alone do not supply that family.

## 3. A necessary covering bound

For any finite S and K,

`|S+K| <= |S| |K|`.

Proof: each pair `(s,k)` produces at most one sum. Therefore covering all
`2^n` unsigned n-bit words requires

`|S_n| |K_n| >= 2^n`.

The bound does not depend on how clever the selector is. Even a B-dependent
choice of fast set must meet the same bound for each fixed B. Meeting this
count is necessary, not sufficient: sums may overlap, correction cost may be
high, and selection may be expensive.

Consequences:

- Trying only k=0 or 1 can cover at most twice as many inputs as the original
  fast family. A tiny exceptional family cannot thereby become universal.
- Offsets in `[-K,K]` enlarge coverage by at most `2K+1`.
- If the fast inputs are p-bit patterns repeated to length n, granting any
  fast algorithm for them, there are at most `2^p` such words. Covering every
  n-bit word then requires at least `2^(n-p)` possible offsets. This rules
  out a polynomial-size offset menu when p is substantially smaller than n.
- Integers described by at most s signed powers among O(n) positions number
  at most `sum_(j=0)^s 2^j binom(O(n),j)`. For s=o(n), this is `2^o(n)`.
  A polynomial number of offsets cannot turn this entire sublinear-weight
  family into all n-bit inputs. The length bound matters; gigantic exponents
  with cancellation are not being granted free representations.

The counting argument rules out these specific coverage proposals, not
all possible structured fast families or all choices of k.

## 4. A stronger result when both pieces use signed shifts

Let w(A) be the minimum number of signed powers of two summing to A. The
canonical non-adjacent form (NAF) attains this minimum; this classical
signed-digit fact is reviewed in
[Heuberger–Prodinger, Section 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/0804.0733v1).

For **every** integer k,

`w(A) <= w(A-k) + w(k)`.

Proof: concatenate optimal signed-power representations of A-k and k.
Their terms sum to A. Equal opposite terms can cancel; equal same-sign terms
can carry to the next power, never increasing term count. The minimum term
count for A cannot exceed the count of this constructed representation.

Thus if both products are evaluated as independent sums of shifted B,
splitting them cannot beat the minimum signed-power term count already
available directly for A. It can substantially improve ordinary unsigned
popcount—such as turning an all-ones word into `2^m-1`—but NAF already includes
that saving. This is a count of flat shifted terms, **not** a lower bound for
general addition chains, factored multiplication, shared subexpressions, FFTs,
or more powerful representations. For example, factoring a coefficient into
two sparse factors permits reuse not modeled by a flat term list.

The exact finite experiments exhaust every A in the indicated domain and
every k from -16 to 16, charging the term count of kB:

| Width bound | A values | Worst optimal signed terms | Splits beating direct optimal signed terms |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 8 | 256 | 5 | 0 |
| 12 | 4,096 | 7 | 0 |
| 16 | 65,536 | 9 | 0 |

We also check 131,328 `(A,k)` pairs with `0 <= A <= 255` and
`-256 <= k <= 256`; none violate the inequality. The general claim comes from
the proof, not from these finite tests.

A useful counterexample is `A=(10101010)_2=170`. Its minimum signed weight is
four. Choosing another k cannot reduce the combined independent signed-term
count below four. Longer alternating patterns give an unbounded family whose
optimal flat representation still has linearly many terms.

## 5. What the small-offset experiment actually covers

Even ignoring the additional cost of the correction, ask whether some
`k in [-16,16]` makes A-k expressible using at most two signed powers:

| Width bound | Covered A values | Coverage |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 8 | 256 / 256 | 100% |
| 12 | 1,428 / 4,096 | 34.86% |
| 16 | 3,656 / 65,536 | 5.58% |

The apparently universal 8-bit success disappears at larger widths. A selector
that looks excellent on tiny examples can therefore fail the intended scaling
test. The DP tables used to exhaust these small domains are charged research
enumeration, not precomputed oracles offered as a fast arbitrary-width algorithm.

There is also a direct carry explanation. If `A=2^t H+L` and
`0 <= k < 2^t`, then

`A-k = 2^t (H-c) + ((L-k) mod 2^t)`, with `c = 1 if L<k else 0`.

The large high part can only become H or H-1. A small offset cannot freely
redesign an arbitrary high-bit pattern. Borrow chains sometimes create useful
runs, but are not a worst-case structural guarantee.

## 6. Why digit splitting and FFT linearity do not close the gap

Choosing k to be a low half of A gives the familiar exact split

`A=2^m H+L`, `AB=(HB)<<m + LB`.

Both shorter-by-full-width products still need evaluation. Splitting both
operands produces four half-size products in the direct scheme; saving a
product requires further algebra, as in classical Karatsuba multiplication.
The identity by itself does not establish a smaller exponent. The standard
arithmetic frameworks, carries, and multiplication reductions are covered in
[Brent–Zimmermann, Modern Computer Arithmetic](https://members.loria.fr/PZimmermann/mca/pub226.html).

For a fresh polynomial/FFT input the distinction is even sharper:

- Subtracting k from the constant polynomial coefficient changes one input
  coordinate; the rest of the fresh transform remains to be computed.
- Subtracting the same k from every time-domain entry changes only the DC
  Fourier coefficient. All other Fourier outputs are unchanged, so this
  operation cannot make their computation disappear.
- If `FFT(A-k)` is already cached, adding a cheap correction is useful, but
  the cached transform is preparation dependent on related data. It does not
  supply the fresh-input worst-case theorem.

The corresponding linear-algebra coverage test is: if "easy A-k" lies in a
subspace U and cheap corrections lie in V, universal coverage requires
`U+V` to be the entire input space. Two small-dimensional shared bases do not
cover arbitrary vectors. This rank observation does not bound all circuit costs.

## 7. Current outcome and next useful version of the idea

We have an exact, bounded-search special-case implementation and an explicit
universal-reduction criterion. On n-bit inputs its detector adds O(n) work;
with a fallback of cost M(n), the worst-case upper bound remains `M(n)+O(n)`.
That preserves the baseline asymptotic order rather than improving it.

The next research target is **an additive cover by a broadly fast family**:
find S and K with full coverage, efficient selection, and fast multiplication
for both pieces. Alternatively, synthesize a single shared graph for the two
products so that additional common work cancels or is reused; independent
flat signed expansions cannot deliver that new saving. These are precise
open tasks for our project. No universal fast k-selection theorem, sub-`n log n`
algorithm, or transfer to the external kappa construction has been established.
