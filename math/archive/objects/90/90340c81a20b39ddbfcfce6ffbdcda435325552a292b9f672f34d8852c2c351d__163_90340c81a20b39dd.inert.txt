# Why the two-stage proof does not immediately imply a three-stage rank bound

For `N=2m`, define a block-diagonal matrix `B` with identical `2x2` blocks

    [[1,1],
     [1,2]]

on coordinate pairs `(0,1),(2,3),...`. This matrix is `1`-quasiseparable in the natural order: any prefix/suffix cut intersects at most one of the blocks, so both off-diagonal cut ranks are <=1. It is also invertible (each block has determinant 1) and is applied with <=O(N) elementary operations.

Let `E={0,2,...,N-2}` and `O={1,3,...,N-1}`. The FOUR half-size matrix blocks

    B[E,E], B[E,O], B[O,E], B[O,O]

all have rank exactly `m`: they equal `I_m`, `I_m`, `I_m`, `2I_m`, respectively.

This counterexample is important because, for a proposed three-stage factorization

    F_N = S3 * P2 * S2 * P1 * S1,

isolating the first/last stage leaves a central submatrix of `S2` indexed by arbitrary permuted row and column subsets. Even if `S2` has quasiseparable cut rank one, ALL FOUR arbitrary half-by-half central submatrices can have full rank simultaneously. Therefore the two-stage proof's ``rank <= r+number of wires retained`` mechanism **cannot simply be reapplied once more** without new multiscale information.

This is not an existence proof for a three-stage Fourier factorization and not a lower bound. It is a concrete exact counterexample to an overly strong *proposed proof technique*.

The right next gate is a multiscale constraint across multiple intervals and permutations, or an exact construction of a new O(N)-cost high-communication operator. Testing only rank of one arbitrary half-submatrix is insufficient.
